💡 Tips for IELTS Listening

  1. In the real exam, the audio is played only once. Take the exercises seriously.
  2. Before listening, read the questions in advance so you know what to look for.
  3. Listen for key words (numbers, names, dates) — they are usually the answers.
  4. Check your spelling. Incorrect spelling means no score in fill-in-the-blank.
  5. Don't ignore articles (a, an, the) next to the blanks — they guide the type of answer.
  6. Use the audio playback button to listen to the transcript like the real exam.
  7. Part 1: Focus on names, numbers, and spelling. Score the most here.
  8. Part 2: Pay attention to maps, plans, or diagrams if shown.
  9. Part 3: Listen for opinions and agreements/disagreements.
  10. Part 4: Hardest part — no breaks. Stay focused and read ahead.
  11. Don't get stuck: If you miss an answer, move on immediately.

Welcome to the Museum of Modern Art's special exhibition on the history of photography. I'm Dr. Helen Park, and I'll be guiding you through the key milestones in this fascinating art form. The earliest known photograph was taken in 1826 or 1827 by a French inventor named Joseph Nicéphore Niépce. His image, called "View from the Window at Le Gras," required an exposure time of approximately eight hours. That's why all the shadows in the image appear on both sides of the buildings — the sun moved during the exposure. In 1839, Louis Daguerre introduced a process that reduced exposure time to just 15 minutes. This was a revolution. The daguerreotype produced incredibly detailed images, but each one was unique — there was no way to make copies. The real breakthrough came in 1851 when Frederick Scott Archer invented the collodion process. This allowed photographs to be reproduced multiple times from a single negative. By 1860, photography studios had opened in virtually every major city in Europe and North America. The 20th century brought dramatic changes. In 1900, the Kodak Brownie camera was introduced at a price of just one dollar, making photography accessible to ordinary people for the first time. Before this, photography had been largely a professional pursuit. The digital revolution began in 1975 when Steven Sasson at Kodak invented the first digital camera. It weighed 3.6 kilograms and could capture images at a resolution of 0.01 megapixels. It took 23 seconds to record an image to a cassette tape. Today, an estimated 1.4 trillion photographs are taken worldwide each year, with smartphones accounting for over 90 percent of all images captured.

Today's lecture examines the growing importance of sustainable architecture in addressing the global climate crisis. I'll cover three main topics: energy-efficient building design, the use of recycled materials, and the concept of biophilic design. First, let me discuss energy efficiency. Buildings account for approximately 40 percent of global carbon emissions. The most effective strategy for reducing this is passive design — the orientation of a building, insulation, natural ventilation, and the use of thermal mass. The Bullitt Center in Seattle, for instance, is described as the greenest commercial building in the world. It generates more electricity than it consumes through its rooftop solar panels, and it captures and treats its own rainwater. The second area is recycled and low-carbon materials. Traditional concrete production is responsible for about 8 percent of global CO2 emissions. Architects are now turning to alternatives such as cross-laminated timber, recycled steel, and hempcrete. Cross-laminated timber, or CLT, is particularly promising — it's as strong as concrete but stores carbon instead of releasing it. A recent study found that building with CLT instead of concrete can reduce a building's carbon footprint by up to 75 percent. Finally, biophilic design integrates natural elements into the built environment. This includes features like living walls, indoor gardens, natural light optimisation, and water features. Research has shown that biophilic design can reduce stress levels by up to 37 percent and increase productivity by 8 percent. The Amazon Spheres in Seattle are a striking example — they house over 40,000 plants from more than 30 countries inside a series of glass domes. The challenge going forward is cost. Sustainable buildings typically cost 10 to 15 percent more to construct than conventional buildings. However, lifecycle cost analyses consistently show that they pay for themselves within 7 to 10 years through energy savings.

Today I want to address a topic that affects every organisation, regardless of size or industry: effective workplace communication. Research consistently shows that poor communication is the number one cause of project failure, accounting for approximately 56 percent of all failed projects according to a study by the Project Management Institute. Let me start with a key distinction — verbal versus non-verbal communication. Studies by Dr. Albert Mehrabian, though often misinterpreted, do highlight an important truth: when words and body language conflict, people trust the body language. In a workplace context, this means that how you say something often matters more than what you say. The first challenge I want to discuss is email overload. The average office worker receives 121 emails per day and spends approximately 28 percent of their workday managing emails. This leads to what researchers call "email fatigue," where important messages get overlooked. A study at the University of British Columbia found that reducing email checking to three times per day improved overall productivity by 18 percent and significantly reduced stress. The second challenge is remote communication. With 58 percent of American workers now working remotely at least part of the time, organisations must adapt their communication strategies. Our research identified three best practices for remote teams: first, establish "core collaboration hours" where all team members are available; second, use video calls instead of phone calls for important discussions — visual cues reduce misunderstandings by approximately 50 percent; third, implement a "no meeting Friday" policy to allow focused work time. The third challenge is cross-cultural communication. In multinational organisations, cultural differences can lead to significant misunderstandings. For example, in many Asian cultures, silence during a meeting indicates thoughtful consideration, while in Western cultures, it may be interpreted as disengagement. The solution lies in cultural intelligence training, which has been shown to improve team performance by 15 percent in diverse workplaces. To summarise, effective communication requires awareness of these challenges and deliberate strategies to overcome them. Organisations that invest in communication training see a return of approximately 7 dollars for every dollar spent, according to a report by the Chartered Institute of Personnel and Development.

The Psychology of Decision Making

Part 4 — Lecture
— / 5 ▼
IELTS Listening Part 4 — The Psychology of Decision Making
Click play to listen to the transcript
📄 Show Transcript (Lecture) ▶
Welcome to today's lecture on the psychology of decision making. My name is Professor Linda Bergström, and I'll be exploring how cognitive biases influence the choices we make every day. Let me begin with one of the most well-documented biases: the anchoring effect. This refers to our tendency to rely too heavily on the first piece of information we encounter. In a classic experiment by Tversky and Kahneman, participants were asked to estimate the percentage of African countries in the United Nations. Before answering, they spun a rigged wheel that landed on either 10 or 65. Those who saw the number 10 estimated an average of 25 percent, while those who saw 65 estimated 45 percent. The arbitrary number completely shifted their judgement. The second bias I want to discuss is the sunk cost fallacy. This is our tendency to continue investing in something because of what we've already spent, rather than evaluating future returns. A study of 1,500 moviegoers found that 54 percent said they would stay to watch a bad movie if they had paid full price, compared to only 26 percent if they had received free tickets. The decision to stay wasn't based on enjoying the film — it was based on justifying the money already spent. The third cognitive bias is the availability heuristic, where we judge the probability of events based on how easily we can recall examples. This explains why many people fear plane crashes more than car accidents, despite car accidents being significantly more common. A survey of 2,000 adults found that 68 percent overestimated the annual number of plane crash fatalities by at least 300 percent. Finally, I'd like to mention choice overload. Research by Sheena Iyengar demonstrated that when a supermarket offered 24 varieties of jam, only 3 percent of shoppers made a purchase. When the display was reduced to 6 varieties, 30 percent bought jam — a tenfold increase. Having too many options actually paralysed decision making. Understanding these biases is crucial because they affect everything from personal finance to medical decisions. A study published in the British Medical Journal found that physicians who were aware of cognitive biases made diagnostic errors 33 percent less often than those who were not trained to recognise them.
